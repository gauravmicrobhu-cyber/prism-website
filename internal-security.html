Pillar One

Internal Security

India's internal security challenges today center less on any single flashpoint and more on how longstanding social fault lines — religious, caste, regional, political, economic — are deliberately exploited and amplified, largely through social media and messaging platforms, rather than created from nothing.

The major fault lines currently being exploited include religious and communal tension; caste, where reservation debates and SC/ST atrocity cases become flashpoints for coordinated narratives pitting caste groups against each other; regional and linguistic divides, particularly around language imposition and "outsider" framing of migrant communities; political and ideological polarization, pushed by organized IT cells and bot networks across the spectrum; and economic distress — farmer struggles, unemployment, inflation — framed to polarize rather than inform.

The mechanisms are consistent across fault lines. WhatsApp remains the primary vector, since its encryption and forwarding scale make content far harder to trace or fact-check than on open platforms. Networks of coordinated inauthentic accounts, some foreign-linked and some domestic, push synchronized hashtags and narratives. Deepfakes and AI-generated content are increasingly used for fabricated speeches or communal provocation clips. Indian agencies and independent researchers have flagged Pakistan-linked and China-linked networks specifically amplifying India's internal fault lines — caste, Kashmir, farmer protests, Northeast insurgency — as part of broader influence operations. And engagement-optimized recommendation algorithms tend to reward emotionally charged, divisive content over neutral reporting.

PRISM's work in this pillar currently includes the Kashmir in the American Record archive, tracing one of these fault lines through the declassified American diplomatic record. Instruments tracking the broader pattern described above — coordinated inauthentic behavior, deepfake circulation, and foreign-linked amplification across caste, region, and religion — are in development.

What the archive has already surfaced

Among the findings: the actual State Department policy paper, signed by Secretary of State George Marshall on the day the UN Security Council first took up Kashmir, showing the plebiscite was America's own preferred outcome from day one — not something imposed by London or the UN. A secret partition proposal Washington and India's own diplomats each considered independently, months apart, neither willing to say so publicly. A Communist-bloc UN commissioner's private counsel to Kashmir's own government against American-backed arbitration. And India's Cabinet privately preparing, in 1950, to accept losing the Kashmir Valley through a plebiscite it expected to lose — a fact that complicates the simplicity of India's later public position.

The work continues session by session, chapter by chapter, through the FRUS volumes that follow — a deliberate, transparent, and slow-built record rather than a finished conclusion.
